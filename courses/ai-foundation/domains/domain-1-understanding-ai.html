1.1 Defining AI

Most modern AI learns patterns from data and uses them to predict, generate, or act. The three outputs matter: predictions (will this email be opened, will this loan default), generated content (text, images, audio, video, code) and actions (navigation rerouting, automated triage). A system that does none of these however clever its rules is not what the syllabus calls AI.

What AI is not is tested as often as what it is. AI is not conscious: no awareness, feelings or intentions, whatever the chat interface implies. It is not infallible: it can be confidently wrong, and fluency is not accuracy. It is not magic: it is pattern learning over large datasets with describable limits. And it is not a person "the AI thought…" is a metaphor, not a fact.

Rule-based systems (human-coded if-then rules, like a tax calculator or spreadsheet formula) are not AI. Learning systems (patterns learned from data, like a spam filter) are. The course also states "simple ≠ wrong": a short, non-technical explanation of AI can be perfectly accurate precision does not require jargon.

1.2 The nesting of terms

The four terms nest, and the course tests the nesting directly. AI is the umbrella. Machine learning is a subset of AI that learns from data. Deep learning is a subset of ML using multi-layer neural networks for patterns too subtle for hand-coded rules. Generative AI and large language models are a subset of deep learning that produces new content. Each term contains the next; none are synonyms.

Why precision matters: vendors call everything "AI-powered", and media use AI loosely for any automation. Being able to label a system precisely lets you evaluate claims instead of repeating them. The syllabus is vendor-neutral and tests the correct term never brand knowledge. Ask the question every time: did this system learn from data, or is it following human-written rules?

1.3 Narrow vs general

Every commercial AI product today is narrow: superb inside its task, helpless outside it. A translation system translates; it cannot drive. Face unlock recognises faces; it cannot write poetry. "Weak AI" in the literature is a description of scope, not an insult narrow systems are extraordinarily powerful, just bounded.

General AI (AGI) is a hypothetical system able to perform any intellectual task a human can. It does not exist; no deployed system is general. Headlines announcing AGI are claims, not facts. Apply the narrow-or-general test to any dramatic claim: name the specific task; change the input to a different kind of task; if the system fails, the claim around it is marketing. Concluding "narrow" is an accurate description, and accurate descriptions are what this certification rewards.

1.4 Everyday AI

Narrow AI is already embedded in routine tools: email spam filtering (learned scoring), search ranking (learned relevance), navigation (learned traffic prediction layered on rule-based routing), recommendations (learned from your history), and photo search (deep learning). Most professionals are already daily AI users, often without noticing.

The data point worth quoting: the ONS found 25% of UK businesses using some form of AI in December 2025, up from 9% in late 2023 and 44% among firms with 250+ staff. The course's job is to turn implicit, invisible use into deliberate, professional use.
