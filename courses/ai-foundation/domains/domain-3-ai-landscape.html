Learning outcomes

By the end of this domain, candidates will be able to:

  • Explain what makes AI generative, as distinct from classifying or labelling
  • Name the major output types: text, image, audio/voice, video and code
  • Describe foundation models and multimodal AI at awareness level
  • Explain the grounding problem and how RAG addresses it, and that RAG reduces rather than removes error
  • Distinguish an AI agent from a single-turn assistant
  • Distinguish the model, the application and the AI-enabled service
  • Select an appropriate category of tool using capability, cost, data sensitivity and organisational approval

What this subject is about

What this domain covers, with teaching notes area by area.

3.1, Generative AI & output types

The core of 3.1 is the distinction: generative AI produces new content, while classifying assigns labels to content that already exists. Photo tagging is classifying (it labels an existing image); writing a poem is generative (the content is new). The five output types are recognition items: text, image, audio/voice, video, and code. Apply the test to anything you meet: is the output new, or a label on old?

3.2, Foundation models & multimodal AI

Awareness level only, recognise this, not in depth. A foundation model is a large, general-purpose model that can be adapted to many different tasks. Multimodal AI handles more than one type of input or output, text and images together, for example. Do not go deeper than the syllabus: recognise the terms and what they point at.

3.3, RAG

The grounding problem: a model on its own guesses from patterns, with no access to your documents or the current web. Retrieval-augmented generation fixes that in four steps: retrieve relevant sources, augment the prompt with them, generate an answer grounded in that material, and (still) verify. The key qualifier: RAG improves freshness and grounding but reduces rather than removes error, the retrieved material can itself be wrong, and the model can still misread it.

3.4, AI agents

An AI agent is a multi-step, tool-using system that pursues a goal: it plans, calls tools, checks results and iterates, unlike a single-turn assistant that answers one prompt. Because agents take actions in the world, they require greater human oversight: the more autonomy, the more checking.

3.5, Model, application & service

Three layers, one distinction to remember. The model is the engine, the learned values that generate. The application is the interface, settings and tools wrapped around the engine. The AI-enabled service is the managed hosted product with support and billing. Open-weight/self-hosted gives you control and data residency, but you own setup and maintenance; hosted is ready to use but governed by the vendor's terms (awareness only).

3.6, Selecting a tool category

Selection is by category, not brand, using four criteria: capability (can it do the task?), cost (what does it really cost at your volume?), data sensitivity (what data will it see, and is that acceptable?), and organisational approval (is it allowed?). Define the task, match the category, check the data rules, get approval. Apply the four criteria, not product knowledge.
