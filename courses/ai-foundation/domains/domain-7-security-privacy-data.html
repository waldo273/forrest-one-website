AI Security, Privacy & Data

Protecting information and recognising AI-enabled risks at a professional-literacy level.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this domain, candidates will be able to:

  • Identify the categories of data that must not be entered into public or unapproved AI tools
  • Apply the default rule: when in doubt, leave it out
  • Describe how third-party AI services may transmit, store or use inputs
  • Explain data retention and residency at a basic level
  • Define shadow AI and describe the risks it creates
  • Recognise deepfakes, impersonation and AI-assisted phishing and social engineering
  • Explain prompt injection and the caution required with untrusted content

What this subject is about

What this domain covers, with teaching notes area by area.

7.1, Protecting information

Four categories of data must never enter public or unapproved AI tools: personal data (names, addresses, medical or financial details), confidential and commercially sensitive information (forecasts, contracts, strategy), credentials (passwords, tokens, API keys), and confidential documents. The default rule, the core core of the domain: when in doubt, leave it out. Assume public, ask first, and remember that one pasted document can leak more than a hundred emails.

7.2, Data handling by AI services

Third-party services may transmit, store, or use your inputs according to their terms: what you type can be retained, and processed or stored in other jurisdictions. Approved tools still have data rules, read what the policy permits. Organisational policy governs AI use; a policy gap is a reason to escalate, not to improvise.

7.3, Shadow AI

Shadow AI is unapproved AI use outside organisational oversight. The risks are data exposure and no audit trail, a single pasted document is a data breach, in any size of organisation. The management answer is cultural as much as technical: approved tools must be good enough that people do not need to bypass policy. Your part: use approved tools, seek approval for new ones, escalate policy gaps.

7.4, AI-enabled threats

Deepfakes, impersonation and AI-assisted phishing are now mainstream attack tools. Context: deepfake files grew from roughly 500,000 in 2023 to an estimated 8 million in 2025; deepfake vishing surged 1,633% in Q1 2025; over 80% of phishing emails contain AI-generated content; the Arup deepfake video-call fraud cost USD 25.6 million. The core defence is a behaviour: spot the trigger (urgency, money, secrecy), pause, verify through a separate trusted channel, then act.

7.5, Prompt injection

Awareness level: malicious instructions can hide inside content the AI processes, documents, emails, web pages. A request like \"ignore previous instructions\" inside a file you ask the AI to summarise is an injection. The defence: treat untrusted content with caution, separate data from commands where you can, and oversee AI actions. Recognition and caution are professional duties, not engineering problems.
