Learning outcomes

By the end of this domain, candidates will be able to:

  • Identify the three sources of bias and explain why bias assessment is a duty in people-affecting uses
  • State the accountability principle and explain why 'the AI did it' is not a defence
  • Describe transparency and explainability at Foundation level
  • Explain why human oversight is necessary and where automated decisions increase risk
  • Apply the principle that AI proposes while a person decides and owns the outcome
  • Decide when AI use must be disclosed
  • Describe the purpose of an organisational AI policy and basic governance
  • Apply the professional decision framework: use / use with verification / escalate / do not use

What this subject is about

What this domain covers, with teaching notes area by area.

8.1, Bias & fairness

Bias has three sources, and the course tests the taxonomy: training data (historical patterns and gaps in what the model learned), design choices (optimisation targets, labelling, inclusion decisions), and deployment context (how and where the system is used, who it is used on, and for what). The impacts are real: hiring, lending, and content all show systematic, unjustified differences. Bias assessment is a professional duty wherever the use affects people, recognition precedes assessment, and you cannot fix bias you cannot see.

8.2, Transparency, accountability & explainability

The non-negotiable line of the domain: the human or organisation remains accountable for AI-assisted decisions, and \"the AI did it\" is not a defence. A bank using AI to decline a loan answers to the customer; an assistant drafting a defamatory line leaves the publisher owning it; a recruiter shortlisting without review owns the outcome. Transparency means openness about when and how AI is used; explainability means being able to say why an output happened. You cannot challenge a decision you cannot explain.

8.3, Human oversight

Oversight is necessary because AI errors are invisible (fluent, confident, wrong) and because automation scales risk, a wrong model decision repeats thousands of times, while a human error stays one event. The syllabus principle: AI proposes while a person decides and owns the outcome. People-affecting decisions are never fully automated without review.

8.4, Disclosure

Three disclosure triggers: where required (legal or contractual), where AI substantially generated the content, or where non-disclosure would mislead. Light-touch assistance needs no disclosure, spell-checking an email or finding synonyms is not material. Substantial generation does, drafting a policy document, generating a customer-facing image. The test: did AI substantially generate the content, and would silence mislead? When in doubt, disclose.

8.5, Policy & governance

An organisational AI policy exists to make AI use safe, consistent and accountable, with three governance components: oversight, accountability and review. The regulatory picture is awareness only: the UK regulates contextually through existing regulators, guided by five principles (safety; transparency; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress); the EU AI Act applies a risk-based framework. Do not over-prepare this area.

8.6, Professional decision framework

The four-way test is the career tool of the course. Use: low-stakes, appropriate, well-understood tasks. Use with verification: higher stakes, where error matters and can be checked. Escalate: uncertainty, you cannot judge the stakes, or the task exceeds your authority. Do not use: high-stakes judgement, authority-based or prohibited contexts. The conservative default is itself core: when unsure, move down a level, be more cautious.
