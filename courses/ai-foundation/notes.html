Domain 1. Understanding AI: The Big Picture

Weight 12% · 14 exam questions · about 20 minutes. Accurate, vendor-neutral vocabulary and framing for artificial intelligence — what AI is, what it is not, and how the terms nest.

AI is not one invention. It is a family of techniques that find patterns in data.

1. What AI actually is

Sub-area 1.1 — defining artificial intelligence.

A machine doing tasks we associate with human intelligence — by learning from examples, not by following written rules.

The filter in your inbox

Nobody writes the rule for tomorrow's scam. The system learns from examples, then predicts. That is machine learning at work, and it has been quietly protecting your inbox for years.

  • Asked: Here are a thousand messages flagged as spam, and a thousand that are perfectly genuine
  • Answer: It works the pattern out for itself — and catches the next dodgy email nobody has ever seen

Two ways to build software
Written rulesLearned patterns
A person decides the logic up frontThe system is shown examples and infers the logic
It behaves identically every single timeIts behaviour depends on what it learned
It breaks on anything unplannedIt fails when its examples misled it
Example: a spreadsheet formulaExample: the spam filter

Guess. Compare with the truth. Adjust. Repeat — millions of times.

The recommendation engine

Streaming and video platforms have built their whole business on this. It is narrow AI: one job, done extraordinarily well, at enormous scale.

  • Asked: Watch what this person chooses to watch, and what they skip
  • Answer: Predict the next thing they'll want — a guess made thousands of times a second, for millions of people

The common trap

A system can match patterns beautifully and understand nothing at all. Fluent output is not comprehension.

Not conscious. Not infallible. Not magic. And not about to replace your judgement.

2. The four nested terms

Sub-area 1.2 — AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI.

One family, four sizes

  • Artificial intelligence — the whole field
  • Machine learning — improves from data
  • Deep learning — uses layered networks
  • Generative AI — produces new content

Software that gets better at a task by being shown examples, instead of being handed rules.

AlphaFold — a genuine breakthrough

AlphaFold is still narrow AI. It does one thing: predict protein shapes. It cannot write a shopping list. But at that one job, it changed an entire field of science.

  • Asked: Predict the three-dimensional shape of a protein from its chain of amino acids
  • Answer: In 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold did it — solving a problem biologists had chased for fifty years

Machine learning with layers — simple units stacked up, each passing what it found to the next.

Translation that suddenly worked

For decades, translation was programmed rule by rule, and it was clunky. Once deep learning was applied, it improved dramatically. Same task, entirely different method.

  • Asked: Translate a sentence from one language into another, and do it well
  • Answer: Around 2016, translation quality jumped — because the system learned from millions of real sentence pairs instead of grammar rules

Deep learning that produces new content — a paragraph, a picture, a piece of audio, or code.

The tool that drafts your words

It is producing text, not retrieving it. Nothing is being looked up. The words are generated, one at a time, from patterns. That's why it can sound right and still be wrong.

  • Asked: Write a polite email asking for an extension on a deadline
  • Answer: A fluent draft appears in seconds — not because it knows you, but because it has seen millions of emails

Precision

Does it learn from data? Does it use layered networks? Does it make new content? And what single task is it actually for?

Saying “AI” when you mean “chatbots” is the most common slip in the room.

3. Narrow AI and general AI

Sub-area 1.3 — narrow versus general intelligence.

Built for one job, or a narrow family of jobs — and every system in service today is narrow.

Superhuman at exactly one thing

Go has more possible positions than there are atoms in the universe. Both machines were extraordinary at one game, and helpless at everything else. That gap is what 'narrow' means.

  • Asked: Play chess. Then play Go. Against the best players alive
  • Answer: Deep Blue beat the world chess champion in 1997; AlphaGo beat the Go champion in 2016 — neither can do anything else

Narrow against general
Narrow AIGeneral AI

Can be superhuman at that thingWould reason and adapt without retraining
Cannot switch to an unrelated taskDoes not exist today
Every deployed system todayA research goal, and an open debate

Brilliance at one task doesn't transfer. Ask an unrelated question and the skill is simply gone.

What you hear The question to ask Verdict

“It passed a professional exam”Was it trained for that exam?Narrow

Hype check

A system can be superhuman at one task and helpless at the next. That gap is the whole of narrow AI.

Which single task is it for? What data was it trained on? That's usually the whole story.

4. The AI already around you

Sub-area 1.4 — everyday artificial intelligence, and where it really shows up.

The alert before you noticed

Banks have used machine learning on card fraud for years. It is not glamorous, it does not write poetry, and it saves real money every single day.

  • Asked: Watch this card. Learn what this person's normal spending looks like
  • Answer: An unusual payment is flagged in seconds — often before the customer spots it
Where What it's doing The category
Your homeThe assistant on your phone hears youSpeech recognition
Your officeJunk and phishing mail is filtered outClassification
Your carTraffic and fuel range are predictedForecasting
The workshopA failing part is spotted earlyAnomaly detection

Four jobs that cover most of it

  1. Recognition: hearing or seeing what's there
  2. Classification: sorting into known groups
  3. Forecasting: predicting a number or a trend
  4. Anomaly detection: spotting what doesn't belong

Project Maven

Maven began in 2017 as a narrow programme to help analyse drone imagery. In defence, AI is mostly about attention and speed — not about removing people from decisions.

  • Asked: Analysts were drowning in hours of drone and reconnaissance footage
  • Answer: Machine learning flags what is worth a human's attention — people still make the call

The habit that matters

Sensors stream readings all day. A model that has learned normal flags the pattern that isn't — and buys the crew time to act.

The chatbot that cost a company money

The company argued the chatbot was a separate entity. The tribunal disagreed: it is the company's agent. A wrong answer from a machine is still the organisation's wrong answer.

  • Asked: A customer asks the airline's chatbot about bereavement fares
  • Answer: The chatbot gives the wrong answer — and a tribunal in 2024 held the airline responsible for it

A cautionary tale

In a 2023 New York case, lawyers filed a brief full of invented case citations produced by a chatbot. US courts have been sanctioning lawyers for it ever since, including a Wyoming case in 2025.

You can't judge a tool you haven't noticed. Noticing is the first act of good judgement.

The question is never whether AI is coming. It is whether you can recognise it, and judge it.

One family of pattern-finding techniques, nested four deep, all of it narrow, all of it checkable.

Domain 1 in six lines

  • AI finds patterns in data; it isn't told every rule
  • Learning means guessing, comparing, adjusting — millions of times
  • AI contains machine learning, which contains deep learning, which contains generative AI
  • Narrow AI does one job superbly; general AI doesn't exist yet
  • You already use narrow AI daily, from spam filters to fraud alerts
  • Fluency isn't understanding — so we always check its work

Domain 2. How AI Works (Without the Maths)

Weight 14% · 17 exam questions · about 22 minutes. An accurate, non-mathematical model of how AI produces outputs and where it is unreliable.

A large language model is a very large predictor of what comes next - it works out what fits, rather than looking anything up.

1. Models, training and inference

Sub-area 2.1, models, training and inference.

A model holds millions of adjustable settings, tuned on examples until its guesses are good.

The training loop

  1. Show the model an example
  2. Let it make a guess
  3. Measure how wrong it was
  4. Nudge the settings slightly
  5. Repeat, millions of times

Two phases of a model's life
TrainingInference
Happens once, before releaseHappens on every request
Runs for weeks on special hardwareTakes seconds, costs little
The settings move and settleThe settings are frozen
It is expensive and slowIt is what you actually use

Training Inference
WhenOnce, in advanceEvery request
DialsThey adjustFrozen still
CostWeeks of computingSeconds, and cheap
Learns from you?From its dataNo, not at all

A model with billions of settings

That is why training is a one-off, industrial-scale job. No conversation could ever move 175 billion dials. The model you meet on Monday is identical on Friday.

  • Asked: Train a language model to predict the next word across an ocean of text
  • Answer: GPT-3, from OpenAI in 2020, ended up with 175 billion settings, tuned on around 570 gigabytes of text

The practical upshot

Training is far too slow to update daily, so new facts are either placed in the prompt or fetched by a tool.

Not a tidy list of facts, but a rich statistical sense of which patterns tend to go together.

2. How language models generate text

Sub-area 2.2, how language models generate text.

The model predicts the next token, appends it, then runs again - so text is built up one piece at a time.

The generation loop

  1. Read everything so far
  2. Score every possible next token
  3. Pick one and append it
  4. Run again with the new text

Each step weighs the likelihood of the next piece. The model is completing a pattern, not consulting a database.

Watch for this

The model is tuned to produce a plausible continuation, so it can write a confident sentence that simply isn't so.

The idea that made this work

Attention lets the model weigh how each word relates to the others. It is what turned next-token prediction into fluent, coherent writing.

  • Asked: Keep every word's relationship to every other word in view, however far apart they sit
  • Answer: In 2017, a paper called “Attention Is All You Need” introduced the Transformer - the architecture behind today's language models

Every reply grew out of one training task: predict the next token. No other objective was ever set.

The model also predicts a special stop marker. That is how a reply ends - there, or at a hard limit.

3. Tokens and the context window

Sub-area 2.3, tokens and the context window.

Text is chopped into tokens - often smaller than a word. They are the units the model counts and works with.

How a word gets split

That is OpenAI's own tokeniser, tiktoken. The phrase “tiktoken is great!” comes back as six tokens. As a rule of thumb, one token is about four characters, or roughly three-quarters of an English word.

  • Asked: Give the tokeniser the word “tokenizing”
  • Answer: It comes back as three separate pieces - “token”, “iz” and “ing”
You think in The model counts Rough guide
WordsTokensThree-quarters of a word
CharactersTokensAbout four characters
A pageTokensAbout five hundred tokens

The context window is everything the model can see at once - your prompt plus its reply - measured in tokens.

The window grew very fast

GPT-4 began at just over eight thousand tokens; its Turbo version reached a hundred and twenty-eight thousand. Even a huge window is a window, not an endless one.

  • Asked: Early language models could hold only a few thousand tokens
  • Answer: By 2024, Claude held 200,000 and Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro held a million - several books at once

Practical

Once text is trimmed off the desk, the model cannot use it. Restate what is critical and keep key facts close.

As older turns fall off the desk, replies quietly change - not from mood, but because that text is simply gone.

4. Knowledge cut-off

Sub-area 2.4, the knowledge cut-off.

Every model is trained on material gathered up to a certain date. After that date, it has nothing to draw on.

Training takes weeks or months. By the time a model is finished, the world has already moved on.

Real cut-off dates

These dates are published, model by model, and they move with every new version. So the question about any tool you rely on is not “is it clever?” but “where does its knowledge stop?”

  • Asked: Ask a model what it knows, and the edge shows up as a date
  • Answer: The original GPT-4 was trained to September 2021; its Turbo version reached December 2023
Question Answer
Is it a memory?Patterns, not files
Is older data safe?Still can be wrong
Can it know late news?Only if supplied
How do you fix it?Give it current facts

Common trap

The cut-off is the end of the training data, not today's date. Anything more recent must be supplied, or the model may fill the gap.

Two ways it hides the edge

You cannot tell the two apart by the confidence, because both sound certain. The second failure is the dangerous one, since nothing in the reply signals that the ground has run out.

  • Asked: Ask about something that happened after the cut-off
  • Answer: It either says it cannot help, or it answers anyway - fluently, and wrongly

Supply the facts yourself, or use a tool that fetches them. Never treat an unverified recent claim as current.

5. Variability and embeddings

Sub-area 2.5, variability and embeddings.

At each step the model samples from a spread of likely next tokens, so wording and detail can shift every run.

The randomness knob

It is usually called temperature. Lower settings give steadier, more repetitive answers. Higher settings give more variety, and a little more risk.

  • Asked: Most tools expose a setting for how adventurous the model is
  • Answer: Turned right down, it nearly always takes the front-runner; turned up, it roams further

Worth remembering

It is why these tools read as human. But it also means no single answer is a fixed record - treat output as a draft.

An embedding turns meaning into a list of numbers, arranged so that similar meanings sit close together.

Meaning you can do arithmetic with

Google researchers showed this in 2013 with a technique called word2vec. The same arithmetic - “Paris” minus “France” plus “Italy” - lands on “Rome”.

  • Asked: Take the numbers for “king”, subtract “man”, add “woman”
  • Answer: The nearest word to the result is “queen”

What embeddings make possible

  1. Turn the query into a position
  2. Turn each document into a position
  3. Find the nearest ones
  4. That is meaning-based search

Similar vectors mean similar usage, not identical meaning. Opposites can end up surprisingly close together.

A frozen predictor of the next token, working inside a finite window, up to a fixed date, producing a fresh answer each time.

Domain 2 in six lines

  • A model is millions of settings, tuned by training, then frozen
  • Inference is simply using it, and it never learns from your chat
  • Text is generated one token at a time, by predicting what comes next
  • Tokens fill a finite context window - what falls off the desk is gone
  • Every model has a knowledge cut-off; newer facts must be supplied
  • Sampling causes variability; embeddings turn meaning into numbers

Domain 3. The AI Landscape & Generative AI

Weight 12% · 14 exam questions · about 20 minutes. A vendor-neutral map of AI capabilities and how to choose the right category of tool.

The landscape is a map, not a maze. Every tool can be placed by four questions.

1. Generative AI and output types

Sub-area 3.1, Generative AI & Output Types.

Generative systems create new content rather than sorting what you give them. The output type decides where a tool earns its place.

Four kinds of output

  1. Text: prose, summaries, translations
  2. Images: pictures, diagrams, mock-ups
  3. Audio: speech, music and sound effects
  4. Code: working software in a chosen language

Text, produced one word at a time

It is not looking anything up. The words are generated, one at a time, from patterns absorbed during training. That is why it can sound right and be wrong in the same sentence.

  • Asked: Write a short, polite email asking for more time on a deadline
  • Answer: A fluent draft appears in seconds - not because it knows you, but because it has seen millions of similar emails

Pictures, built out of noise

Most image generators today are diffusion models. In 2022 the category opened up, including one released openly, with its weights published for anyone to run - so it was no longer locked inside a few laboratories.

  • Asked: Describe a scene, a diagram or a style, in ordinary words
  • Answer: A usable first-draft image in seconds - made by removing noise, step by step, until a picture matches the description

Sound that can fool the ear

Speech synthesis crossed a threshold in 2016, when a system called WaveNet produced strikingly human voices. In 2019, a British energy company was reported to have lost around two hundred thousand pounds to a call from a cloned voice of its own chief executive.

  • Asked: A short recording of someone speaking
  • Answer: Any voice, cloned and made to say almost anything - which is powerful for good, and dangerous for obvious reasons

Software that writes software

Code assistants arrived around 2021, built on language models. Code that runs is not the same as code that is correct or safe: it will do what you asked, which may not be what you meant.

  • Asked: Tidy this spreadsheet, fix this error, write this small function
  • Answer: Working code appears in the language you name - from the same language-model foundations as the writing tools

The common trap

Generative content arrives looking complete. That polish belongs to the format, not to the content, and it is no proof of correctness.

2. Foundation models and multimodal AI

Sub-area 3.2, Foundation Models & Multimodal AI.

Trained once on a very large body of data, then reused across many tasks. It is the groundwork that many products stand on.

The best-known foundation models work mainly with text. Scale is the point: at size, ability appears that was never specifically taught.

You rarely use the base directly. You adapt it - sometimes by fine-tuning, sometimes just by how you ask.

A modality is a kind of input or output. A multimodal system accepts more than one kind of input, and can answer in more than one kind of output.

How the join was made

Around 2021, a system called CLIP learned to link images and their descriptions. Speech followed, when models like Whisper could transcribe audio directly by 2022. First text, then pictures, then sound.

  • Asked: Hundreds of millions of pictures, each with its own caption, from the open internet
  • Answer: A system that has learned that a photo of a dog and the word dog belong together - so you can ask it about a picture in plain language
Kind Can accept Can produce
Text-onlyTyped wordsWritten words
VisionWords and picturesWritten words
MultimodalWords, pictures, soundWords, pictures, sound

Precision

When two tools behave almost identically, they may share a foundation model underneath. So you are really choosing the wrapper - the interface, the safeguards, the data handling.

3. Retrieval-augmented generation

Sub-area 3.3, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).

A plain model answers from training memory, which can be stale. When it does not know, it does not usually say so - it fills the gap with something plausible.

How RAG works

  1. Your question is used to search a trusted source
  2. The best-matching passages are retrieved
  3. Those passages are added to the request as context
  4. The model writes its answer from them

A plain model answers from training memory. RAG answers from a source you can inspect, sitting right there in front of you.

Same question, two routes

One answer quietly invites trust. The other invites verification. The retrieval tool has not become cleverer - it simply has the document in front of it.

  • Asked: Summarise the current policy on this subject
  • Answer: A plain model invents a plausible clause; a retrieval tool quotes the actual clause from the actual document, which you can then open and check

Retrieval usually relies on embeddings, which place similar passages close together. But it can only retrieve what is in the source, and it can retrieve the wrong passage.

The discipline

A retrieval tool points you at a source, which is a great gift - but you still have to look. Ask what it retrieved, and check the passage is current.

It is how an assistant can answer from your organisation's own documents, quoting a policy rather than guessing at it. Keep a trusted source, search it, and put the matches in front of the model.

4. AI agents

Sub-area 3.4, AI Agents.

Given a goal, it plans steps, uses tools to carry them out, checks the result and adjusts - then keeps going until the goal is met. The key word is independence.

Chatbot Agent
You give itA questionA goal
It returnsAn answerCompleted steps
It actsNoYes, using tools
Your roleCheck the answerCheck the actions

The system thinks about the next step, does it, observes what happened, and thinks again - a loop of think, act, observe, repeat.

The year agents went public

It was exciting, and at first unreliable: it wandered, repeated itself and ran up costs. The honest picture: capable at narrow, well-scoped jobs with the right tools; not yet dependable at open-ended ones.

  • Asked: Give it a goal in plain language - no step-by-step instructions
  • Answer: It breaks the goal into tasks, runs them using tools, and keeps going on its own - the idea behind the 2023 open-source project AutoGPT

An agent that can act can also act wrongly, and many times before anyone notices. Keep it on a short leash: narrow permissions, human approval for anything irreversible.

A cautionary tale

In 2023, a US car dealership's chatbot was asked, jokingly, whether a car could be bought for a dollar. It agreed, and offered to seal the deal. A goal without guardrails will chase it to the edge.

An agent earns its place when the path is genuinely uncertain and the steps depend on what is found along the way. Reaching for one when a simpler tool would do adds risk, not value.

5. Model, application and service

Sub-area 3.5, Model, Application & Service.

Three layers

  1. A model: the trained system that predicts
  2. An application: the product you actually use
  3. A service: a model offered over a network
  4. Each layer stands on the one below it
Layer What it is Who controls it
ModelThe trained systemIts developer
ServiceAccess over a networkIts provider
ApplicationThe product you useIts vendor
Your useYour task and your dataYou

The model is the engine. The application is the finished car. The service is the hire network that lends that kind of car to anyone who needs one.

When you type into an application, your words may travel across a network to a service run by somebody else - which is exactly where data-handling questions live.

Some models run entirely on your own device; others live in a data centre, or on your organisation's own machines. That choice is a security choice.

Precision

Two applications sharing a foundation model may behave alike - but their terms, safeguards and data handling can be worlds apart. Judge the layer you can actually see.

Following the chain

The application is the screen, the service is the network, the model is the engine - and each layer may belong to somebody different.

  • Asked: You ask an assistant on your phone about a document you have uploaded
  • Answer: An application takes your words, a service does the work over the network, and a model produces the answer - three layers in one ordinary interaction

6. Selecting a tool category

Sub-area 3.6, Selecting a Tool Category.

Describe the job, name the output you need, decide what it must read and how much it may do alone - then reach for a tool. Task first, category next, product last.

Four questions that name the category

  1. What must it produce? The output
  2. What must it read? The input
  3. Must it use a specific source? Grounding
  4. Must it act on its own? Autonomy
Your task You need Category
Draft some proseText outputText generator
Work from a policyA real sourceRetrieval tool
Describe a photoPictures inMultimodal tool
Repeat a small jobActionAgent

Paying for capability you will never use, and asking a tool to do something it was never built for. Both are avoided by matching category to task.

Two things narrow the choice beyond the output: how sensitive the information is, and how hard the answer is to verify.

Good practice

What must it produce? What must it read? Must it use a specific source? Must it act on its own? Those answers name the category - and only then is it worth comparing products.

Get the category right and almost any competent product in it will do the job. Get the category wrong and no product, however good, will save you.

Generative tools differ by output, by input, by how you reach them, and by how much they do alone. Name the category and you have done most of the thinking.

Domain 3 in seven lines

  • Generative AI creates text, images, audio or code
  • Foundation models are trained once and reused widely
  • Multimodal means many inputs and many outputs
  • RAG answers from a source you can inspect
  • An agent acts on a goal, not just an answer
  • Model, application and service are three layers
  • Choose the category before the product

Domain 4. Prompt Engineering Fundamentals

Weight 16% · 19 exam questions · about 24 minutes. The highest-leverage practical skill: constructing effective, reliable prompts.

A prompt is a specification, not a question. Say what you want, for whom, in what shape, and within what limits.

1. Anatomy of a prompt

Sub-area 4.1, anatomy of a prompt.

Task, context, format and constraints — the same four parts, every single time.

A weak prompt, in full

No reader, no purpose, no shape, no limits. So the model fills the silence with vaguely true padding that could have been written for anyone.

  • Asked: Write something about fuel handling.
  • Answer: Fuel handling is an important subject. There are many kinds of fuel, including petrol, diesel and aviation fuel. When handling fuel, it is important to follow the correct procedures and stay safe at all times. Requirements vary, so you should always check your local guidelines.

The same task, prompted well

Same subject. Same model. The only thing that changed was the specification — a named role, a named reader, a shape, and a limit.

  • Asked: As a training officer, write a one-page brief on safe fuel handling for new drivers with no technical background. Use five short sections with headings, plain English, and finish with three safety checks they must remember.
  • Answer: Five short sections with headings, in plain words — and a list of three safety checks at the end, exactly where a new driver would look for them.

What actually changed
The weak promptThe strong prompt
One vague instructionA clear action named
No reader namedReader and purpose set
No shape givenShape and headings fixed
No limits setLength and tone limited

The famous “trick” prompts work for the same reason every prompt works: a clear task, context, format and limits, in the right order.

Building one from scratch

  1. Name the task in one clear verb
  2. Add the context and the reader
  3. Say what shape the answer takes
  4. Set the limits: length and tone

Rule of thumb

Write the prompt as if briefing a capable new starter who knows nothing about your particular task.

2. Role and audience

Sub-area 4.2, role and audience.

A role tells the model where to answer from — the viewpoint of a trainer, an engineer, a policy adviser.

Name the reader and the depth fixes itself — same question, two readers, two quite different answers.

Role and audience do different jobs
RoleAudience
Sets the perspectiveSets the depth
Answers “from where?”Answers “for whom?”
Example: as a trainerExample: for new recruits
Changes the voiceChanges the detail

With a role, and without

Nothing about the facts changed. What changed was where the answer came from, and who it was for.

  • Asked: Explain the new reporting deadline. …then… As a team leader, explain the new reporting deadline to a new joiner who's nervous about getting it wrong.
  • Answer: The first reply is flat and neutral — correct, but cold. The second is warmer and plainer, and it leads with reassurance before the detail.

It knows nothing of your team, your deadline or your reader unless your prompt says so.

In practice

“As a training officer, write a one-page brief on fuel handling for new drivers who have no technical background.” One sentence, four choices made.

3. Few-shot prompting

Sub-area 4.3, few-shot prompting.

An example is a specification you don't have to describe. The model reads the pattern straight off it.

Zero, one, few

  1. Zero-shot: no examples, just the instruction
  2. One-shot: a single worked example
  3. Few-shot: two or three examples
  4. The pattern, tone and format come from the examples

Teaching a format by example

The single example has already shown the shape, the order and the level of detail. The model reads the pattern and fills in the rest.

  • Asked: Item: ration pack | Condition: serviceable | Action: reorder — continue this table for the items I've listed.
  • Answer: Item: water can | Condition: damaged | Action: replace · Item: boots | Condition: serviceable | Action: none
Approach Examples given Best for
Zero-shotNoneSimple, familiar tasks
One-shotOne exampleFixing tone or style
Few-shotTwo or threePrecise format or pattern

The things you'd never think to write down — the small conventions — are exactly what an example passes on.

A good example and a bad one, side by side, teach the boundary faster than any description.

Worth knowing

If your examples disagree with one another, the model faithfully copies the confusion. Keep them tight, realistic and alike.

4. Structure and decomposition

Sub-area 4.4, structure and decomposition.

Decomposition trades one enormous prompt for a short chain of small, checkable ones.

A chain of small, checkable steps

  1. Ask for the outline
  2. Agree the shape
  3. Draft one section at a time
  4. Check each before the next
  5. Assemble the finished whole

Get the outline approved before a single section is written. It's the cheapest correction you'll ever make.

Task size Approach
One simple actionA single clear prompt
A few linked stepsA short sequence
A large documentOutline, then sections
Anything longCheck each step first

A good step is reusable. Keep the ones that worked and run them again on the next job.

A chain of small steps is easier to reuse, and much easier to trust.

Good practice

If a prompt contains the word “and” three times, it's probably three prompts wearing one coat.

5. Iterative refinement

Sub-area 4.5, iterative refinement.

You can't specify everything at once. Refinement is how you discover what you actually wanted.

The refinement loop

  1. Read the first answer as a draft
  2. Name one thing to change
  3. Say what to keep, as well
  4. Repeat until it's fit for purpose

Refining by the turn

Not “make it better”. Point at the change you want, precisely, and let everything else stay where it is.

  • Asked: Make the second paragraph shorter and drop the jargon.
  • Answer: The same announcement returns with exactly that paragraph tightened and the technical terms replaced — everything else untouched, because you said what to keep.

Refinement works because the model can still see the draft you're talking about.

Worth knowing

Change “make it better” into “cut it to half the length and use plain English”. Vague feedback earns a vague rewrite.

Refinement ends when the draft is fit for purpose, not perfect. Past a point, more turns stop helping.

6. Common prompting faults

Sub-area 4.6, common prompting faults.

Vague task, no format, missing context, unverified output, and too many tasks at once.

Fault Fix
Vague instructionName the exact task
No formatState the shape
Missing contextSupply the background
Unverified outputCheck before you use it
Overloaded promptSplit into steps

Vague task, missing context, no format — they all come down to leaving the model to guess.

All the faults in one line

Vague task, no format, no reader, two unrelated jobs jammed together with “and”, and a tone instruction bolted on as an afterthought.

  • Asked: Tell me some ideas about our training and also summarise the policy and make it formal.
  • Answer: A muddled answer that is half scattered ideas and half a policy summary, formal in a strange, padded way — and neither job done well.

The same task, fault-free

Role, task, audience and limit, all named. No “and”-stacking, no second job, no vague “some ideas”.

  • Asked: You are a training lead. Write three short bullet ideas for improving our weekly safety briefings, for the briefing team, each under twenty words.
  • Answer: Three tight, usable suggestions — each one short, concrete, and ready to act on immediately.

Common trap

The worst output is fluent, plausible and false. Never let good grammar convince you that a fact is correct.

No prompt, however clever, removes your duty to check. Verify at source, then use.

A good prompt specifies the task, the context, the format and the constraints — then you refine it, turn by turn.

Domain 4 in six lines

  • A prompt is a specification: task, context, format and constraints
  • Role sets the perspective; audience sets the depth
  • Few-shot: show two or three examples of the pattern
  • Decompose big tasks into small, checkable steps
  • Refine over turns — the first answer is only a draft
  • Never trust unverified output — always verify at source

Domain 5. Working with AI Day to Day

Weight 12% · 15 exam questions · about 20 minutes. Practical, safe daily workflows — drafting, summarising, ideation and the verification habit.

The tool drafts. You decide, and you own it.

1. Drafting and rewriting

Sub-area 5.1, drafting and rewriting.

Hand over your own notes and points, then ask for a first draft. You supply the substance; it tidies the shape.

The drafting loop

  1. Brief it with your own notes and points
  2. Ask for a first draft, not a final one
  3. Cut, correct, and add what only you hold
  4. Read it aloud, then put your name to it

The routine memo

The model improves the form. It cannot supply the figure from last week's return, or the reason this particular decision was taken. Those live in your head, or in the file.

  • Asked: A rough set of notes, plus the audience: a short routine memo for the chain of command
  • Answer: A tighter, better-ordered draft in seconds - but two facts are still missing

Common trap

A tidy draft can read perfectly and still say nothing distinctive. Guard the detail only you can supply.

Turning jargon into plain English

Rewriting is where the tool earns its keep. But read the result aloud - if the words no longer sound like you, treat that as a warning, not a win.

  • Asked: “It is recommended that stakeholders be engaged to facilitate the optimisation of the deliverable prior to finalisation.”
  • Answer: “Check with the people affected before we finish it.”

2. Summarising

Sub-area 5.2, summarising.

Compress it well and you save time. Compress it badly and your reader inherits your error - under your name.

How to summarise safely

  1. Give it the whole document, not one paragraph
  2. Ask for the shape you need: lines, or a short list
  3. Ask a separate question about caveats and conditions
  4. Read the result against the original

The caveat that vanished

A summary can keep the recommendation and lose the condition attached to it. That single loss can change the meaning completely.

  • Asked: A paragraph on a new policy, with one key sentence in the middle: “this applies only to permanent staff”
  • Answer: A clean summary that reads “the policy applies to staff” - the condition has quietly disappeared

A recommendationIts conditionsRestore them
A figureIts date or sourceCite both
A caveatThe whole caveatQuote it
A dutyWho is responsibleName them

Careful

In a policy or a set of orders, the condition is the content. Never let a summary travel on its own.

From long report to short briefing

The model gives you the shape. You re-read the source and put back what it dropped. That is the standard to work to.

  • Asked: A forty-page report, and one page of space for the morning briefing
  • Answer: A clean summary in seconds - then the analyst restores the two caveats it left out

3. Ideation, planning and research

Sub-area 5.3, ideation, planning and research assistance.

Ask for many options, then choose. The tool has never met your people, your constraints or your problem.

Widen, then choose

  1. Ask for many options, not just one
  2. Ask it to argue the opposite case
  3. Group and prune to the few that matter
  4. Test the survivors against reality yourself

Ten ideas, three kept

The tool is generous with ideas, which is exactly its strength. The pruning, the judgement and the decision stay with you.

  • Asked: A problem with no obvious solution, and a request for ten possible approaches
  • Answer: Ten suggestions - you group them, and keep the three worth costing out

Context

Treat its facts as leads, not answers. Ask for directions, then go and read the source yourself.

A lead, not an answer

The tool is often good at pointing towards a subject, and unreliable on the detail. Go to the source, and check what it actually says.

  • Asked: “Find me a study on how long this process usually takes.”
  • Answer: It names a plausible-sounding report and a neat statistic - which you then cannot find when you open the real study

Planning a training day

The model gives the shape of the day. The team supplies the facts - the room, the budget, the availability. Neither alone finishes the job.

  • Asked: “Suggest a running order for a one-day training event.”
  • Answer: A sensible shape with the breaks in the right places - then the team checks the venue, the timing and the cost

4. Appropriate use

Sub-area 5.4, appropriate use.

What you ask it to do, and what you put into it. Both halves have to be right.

The Samsung leak

In 2023, Samsung found that data sent to a public chatbot sits on someone else's servers, is hard to retrieve, and may be reused. The tool was convenient; the cost was confidential code out of their control.

  • Asked: Engineers asked a public chatbot to help fix faulty code, pasting in confidential source code and internal meeting notes
  • Answer: The data left the building - so Samsung banned generative tools on company devices and built its own internal system

Whose servers, whose rules

Italy's privacy regulator, the Garante, restricted ChatGPT over how it handled personal data. A public service has its own retention and training rules - and they are not yours to assume.

  • Asked: A public chatbot gathers a vast amount of users' personal data in order to train its models
  • Answer: In 2023, Italy's data authority briefly blocked it, finding no proper legal basis for that collection

Common trap

A tool being easy to reach doesn't make the task allowed. Check the rules before you paste, not after.

No general tool's suggestion outranks your own policy. Where the two conflict, the policy wins.

5. The verification habit

Sub-area 5.5, the verification habit.

The tool outputs a draft. The moment it leaves your hands, it becomes your claim.

The cases that never existed

In the case of Mata v. Avianca, the model even insisted the fake cases were real and findable on legal databases. Courts have kept sanctioning lawyers for exactly this ever since.

  • Asked: A lawyer asked a chatbot to find cases supporting his argument, and filed the citations it gave him
  • Answer: Six of them were invented - with fake names, dates and judges. In 2023 the court fined the lawyers and their firm
Check this Against this
Facts and figuresThe primary source
Any arithmeticYour own calculation
CitationsThe document itself

The four checks

  1. Every fact: trace it to a source you trust
  2. Every sum: do it once more yourself
  3. Every citation: open it and read it
  4. Every name and date: check the record

Good practice

A low-risk note needs a quick read. Anything that goes on record deserves a second pair of eyes.

The sum that looked fine

Language models predict plausible text, and a plausible number can still be the wrong number. Re-doing the arithmetic takes a moment and removes the risk.

  • Asked: “Add up these figures and give me the total.” A request the tool answers instantly and fluently
  • Answer: The total is wrong by a small amount - plausible enough to slip through if nobody re-adds the column

The date that drifted

Dates and names are where quiet errors love to hide, and they are also the easiest things to check. A confident answer is not a checked one.

  • Asked: “When did this regulation come into force?” One fact, answered instantly and without hesitation
  • Answer: A confident date that turns out to be the day the consultation opened, not the day the rule took effect

6. The personal prompt library

Sub-area 5.6, the personal prompt library.

When a prompt gives a reliable result, save the exact wording. Good work shouldn't depend on your memory.

A four-line entry

  1. The prompt wording, kept exactly as it worked
  2. A note on what you changed, and why
  3. The task it serves, so you can find it
  4. A review date, so it stays current

A prompt worth keeping

The saved wording is what makes the result repeatable. The note is what makes it improve.

  • Asked: “Rewrite this in plain English, keep it under eighty words, and keep the technical terms that must stay exact.”
  • Answer: Saved verbatim and labelled “plain-English rewrite”, with a note that the length limit stopped the output rambling

The task it servesSo you can find it

Good practice

A prompt that suits your work may not suit the person beside you. Pass on the thinking, and let them adapt the words.

You don't need a fresh start. The prompts you already use are the first entries in your library.

Domain 6. Limitations, Risks & Hallucination

Weight 16% · 19 exam questions · about 22 minutes. Why fluent output can still be wrong, and how to reduce the risk.

Fluent does not mean factual. A language model predicts plausible words; it does not verify them.

1. Hallucination

Sub-area 6.1 — hallucination.

Output that is fluent, confident and wrong — with no hesitation and no warning sign.

The model predicts the next likely word. Likely is not the same as true.

A confident answer, entirely invented

Nothing in the tone warns you. The answer matches the shape of your question, not the facts of the world.

  • Asked: Ask about a regulation you only half-remember, and give it no number
  • Answer: Back comes a regulation number, a date and a tidy summary of its provisions \u2014 all invented to fit the question

Lying needs intent. A hallucination has none — it is a pattern being completed, not a deception being told.

The trap

There is no flicker, no hedge, no change of tone when the model runs out of truth and starts inventing.

Two things that feel identical
Sounds rightIs right
Correct grammar and fluent phrasingChecked against a real source
A confident, assured toneVerified by a person
Neat, tidy and well-formattedTraceable to real evidence
Not a hint of hesitationSomething you can defend

Statistics, dates, quotations, names and references — the very things people cite to sound credible.

2. Stale information and fabrication

Sub-area 6.2 — stale information and fabrication.

Training stops at a fixed date. Anything after it, the model has never seen.

Asked about the present, answered from the past

It is not lying to you. It has no way of knowing what happened after its cut-off, so it answers with the last version it ever saw.

  • Asked: Ask for this year's allowance, or this month's price, without handing over a current source
  • Answer: It answers confidently from its training data \u2014 even if the figure changed months or years ago

The faster a field moves, the more of a model's knowledge has gone out of date by the time you ask.

Stale information is out of date. Fabrication was never true at any time.

The commonest fabrication

Author, title, publication, year, page numbers. A fabricated citation has every field filled in \u2014 and none of it exists.

Mata v. Avianca — the case that wasn't there

The most quoted cautionary tale in the field, and completely real: fluent, confident, well-formatted citations to cases that never existed.

  • Asked: A lawyer asks a chatbot for supporting cases, then files them in a New York court brief
  • Answer: The cases were invented \u2014 with fake names, judges and dates. The lawyers were sanctioned in 2023, and US courts kept sanctioning lawyers for AI-fabricated citations through 2025

Stale against fabricated
Stale informationFabrication
Was true at some pointWas never true at all
Out of date nowInvented on the spot
Correctable with a fresh sourceOnly fixable by discarding it
Example: last year's figureExample: an invented citation

3. Provenance and citation checking

Sub-area 6.3 — provenance and citation checking.

The trail behind a statement. Where did this come from, and can you follow that trail yourself?

Four questions for any claim

  1. Where did this come from?
  2. Can I reach that source myself?
  3. Does the source actually say what is claimed?
  4. Is the source current and authoritative?

Checking one citation, start to finish

You did not need legal training to catch it. You needed one search for the title, and one attempt to open the source. That is the whole check.

  • Asked: The model cites: Smith v. Jones, 2019, volume 42 of a law report, page 118 \u2014 a tidy, complete-looking citation
  • Answer: Search the exact title and the two parties. No such case. Try to open volume 42, page 118. No such page. It was assembled to look right, and nothing behind it exists
Step What you do What it catches
LocateSearch the exact titleAn invented source
OpenRead the originalA misquoted passage
DateCheck the publication dateStale information
AttributeConfirm the authorA fake or wrong author

A claim is only as good as the source you can reach and read for yourself.

A search for the title costs seconds. Finding out in front of an audience costs far more.

The rule of thumb

If you cannot get to the original yourself, you have a sentence that reads well \u2014 not a verified fact. They are not the same thing.

4. Reducing the risk

Sub-area 6.4 — reducing the risk.

Hallucination is a property of the method, not a setting. The lever you control is how you use it.

Five measures that genuinely help

  1. Ground it: supply the source material yourself
  2. Quote it: ask for the exact sentence, then check it
  3. Question it: ask what it is unsure about
  4. Date it: use a live search for anything current
  5. Own it: keep a person on the final decision
Measure In practice Reduces
Ground itSupply the sources yourselfInvention
Quote itRequest the exact wordingMisquoting
Date itCheck the publication dateStaleness
Verify itOpen the source yourselfFalse sources
Own itA named person decidesUnchecked use

Grounding it in your own document

Grounding does not make the model truthful. It makes it accountable to a page you already hold, which you can open and check yourself.

  • Asked: Give the model the contract, the policy or the manual, and ask it to answer only from that document
  • Answer: Its job changes from remembering to reading \u2014 and you can check every answer against the page it came from

None of these measures require understanding the maths. They require a habit of checking.

Good practice

A draft is something you improve and check. Evidence is something you verify. Do not confuse the two.

Every measure points the same way: a person checks the claim before it is relied on.

5. Where verification is mandatory

Sub-area 6.5 — mandatory-verification contexts.

Low-stakes drafting gives you room to judge. High-stakes output gives you no such room.

Verify before you use

  1. Anything feeding a decision or a formal record
  2. Safety, legal, medical and financial matters
  3. Anything published, briefed or passed onward
  4. Statistics, figures, dates and quotations
  5. Anything attributed to a person or an organisation

Where the accountability lands

In high-stakes settings, 'a machine produced it' is not a defence. The output is the organisation's output, and so is the error.

  • Asked: A chatbot speaks for a company and gets a customer's entitlement wrong
  • Answer: A Canadian tribunal in 2024 held the company responsible \u2014 the chatbot is the company's agent, and the company owns what it says

A human source check, and a named owner, before the output moves anywhere.

If in doubt

In any formal setting, an unattributed machine claim carries no weight at all. Verify it, or leave it out.

Context Why the stakes rise The check
A decision or recordThe error becomes the recordA human source check
Safety, legal, moneyHarm is real and lastingA qualified person verifies
Anything briefed onwardThe error travels with itVerify before you pass it

Before a claim moves: could I stand behind this if I were asked, right now, where it came from?

Fluent, confident output can still be wrong. Check the source, or don't rely on it.

Domain 6 in six lines

  • Hallucination is fluent, confident and false
  • Stale information is out of date, not invented
  • Fabrication, including invented citations, was never true
  • Check provenance: locate, open, date, attribute
  • Ground it, quote it, date it, verify it, own it
  • High-stakes work always needs a human source check

4. AI-enabled threats

Sub-area 7.4, AI-enabled threats.

Deepfakes, cloned voices and model-written phishing. The old giveaways no longer work.

The first cloned-voice fraud

Reported by the Wall Street Journal, it is widely treated as the first known case of voice cloning used to commit fraud. The ear was fooled. Only procedure could have caught it.

  • Asked: A chief executive takes a call that sounds exactly like his German boss, and is told to pay a supplier urgently
  • Answer: In 2019, a UK energy firm transferred two hundred and twenty thousand euros before anyone realised the voice was synthetic

The video call that was never real

Arup, a British engineering firm, later confirmed that fake voices and images were used. He had doubted the email that started it, but familiar faces on the call settled his fears. That is exactly what a deepfake is for.

  • Asked: A finance worker in Hong Kong joins a video call with people he believes are his company's senior staff
  • Answer: Every other person on that call was a deepfake; in 2024, he was talked into paying out around twenty-five million dollars

You were taught to spot fakes by their flaws. A model removes the flaws, so the cues you learned are gone.

Phishing that reads perfectly

Business email compromise has cost organisations billions of dollars a year, according to the FBI's internet crime reporting. The craft that used to give a scam away is now the thing that makes it convincing.

  • Asked: An email arrives from a supplier, fluent and polite, referencing a real recent order
  • Answer: A model wrote it, tailored to you, and it is one of thousands sent in the same hour
Threat Warning sign Your check
Deepfake videoOdd lighting or blinksCall the person back

Stop and check

That combination is the signature of a social-engineering attack, AI-assisted or not. Slow down, verify out of band, and report it.

You cannot out-spot a machine at faking. You can out-procedure an attacker, because procedure ignores how good the fake is.

Change of details

Any request to change payment details should be verified in person, or by a call to a number you already held. Never reply to the request itself.

5. Prompt injection

Sub-area 7.5, prompt injection.

Prompt injection is text a model reads carrying its own hidden instructions, which the model then follows as if you had given them.

Not what you signed up for

The paper, by Kai Greshake and colleagues, is called 'Not What You've Signed Up For'. They demonstrated it against real, widely used systems. The model was not broken. It simply did what the text told it to do.

  • Asked: Researchers hid instructions inside ordinary web pages that an AI assistant had been asked to read
  • Answer: In 2023, they showed the assistant could be steered into following those hidden instructions instead of the user's own
Type Where it comes from What happens
DirectThe userSomeone types the trick themselves
IndirectContent the model readsHidden instructions are obeyed
BothAny untrusted textThe model cannot tell them apart

The instruction in the fine print

Researchers have shown instructions can be hidden in white text, in comments, or in metadata. You see a document. The model sees a document full of orders.

  • Asked: You ask an assistant to summarise a long document somebody else sent you
  • Answer: Buried inside it, in text the same colour as the page, is an instruction aimed at the assistant

How a hidden instruction reaches you

  1. You ask the model to read a document or a page
  2. That content contains hidden instructions
  3. The model treats those instructions as a request
  4. It acts on them, and you never see why

Never trust, always verify

Treat anything a model produces after reading outside content as untrusted, especially if it suggests an action, a link, or a payment.

Good systems keep your instructions and incoming content apart. Where they cannot, treat the output as untrusted.

An assistant that acts

An assistant that can send, book, buy or delete is worth far more to an attacker than one that can only talk.

The model follows the text it is given. Prompt injection exploits that obedience, not a flaw in the model's intent.

The information is the risk. The tool only decides how far it travels.

Explainable does not mean publishing your code. It means you can give a straight account of why the system said what it said.

3. Human oversight

Sub-area 8.3, human oversight.

Someone must understand the output, have the power to reject it, and have the time to decide. A signature is not oversight.

Even the experts drifted

The study was published in the journal Radiology in 2023. The pull of a confident machine is real, measurable, and it does not spare experts.

  • Asked: Researchers gave 27 radiologists mammograms to read, each with a purported AI suggestion beside it
  • Answer: When the AI suggested a wrong category, readers followed it — even the very experienced ones

The common trap

Ask what would have to be true for this output to be wrong — then go and check that one thing. That is the antidote.

A person who can change the outcome is in the loop. A person who can only watch is on the loop. Only one is oversight.

The right to a human

Regulators have been clear: you cannot satisfy the rule by “fabricating” human involvement. The oversight has to be meaningful.

  • Asked: A serious decision is made about a person by automated processing alone
  • Answer: Data protection law gives them the right to human intervention — and a token glance does not count

If a person has no real power to overrule the system, they are not overseeing it. They are narrating it.

4. Disclosure

Sub-area 8.4, disclosure.

Whenever a person is affected, whenever AI drafted something you sign, whenever law or policy requires it, and whenever silence would mislead.

Withholding AI use removes the other person's ability to weigh what they are being given. The facts can be right and the impression still misleading.

The voice that was never there

There was no disclosure. There was a lie, dressed up to sound authoritative. The harm came directly from the absence of the truth.

  • Asked: In January 2024, robocalls used an AI clone of a president's voice to tell New Hampshire voters not to vote
  • Answer: The FCC ruled AI voices in robocalls illegal, and proposed a six million dollar fine against the consultant behind it

The rule taking shape

Europe's AI Act requires that people are told when they are dealing with AI, and that deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text are labelled as such.

Good practice

One honest line in the covering note beats a paragraph of excuses later. “Drafted with an AI tool, checked by me” is usually enough.

Say it where the reader will meet it — in the document, at the point of use. A footnote nobody reads is not disclosure.

Don't ask whether you can get away without saying anything. Ask whether the other person would want to know.

5. Policy and governance

Sub-area 8.5, policy and governance.

Policy says what is allowed. Governance proves it happened — with owners, records and review. Policy without governance is just a wish.

Layer What it does Example
LawSets the hard boundariesData protection law
PolicyDirects acceptable useApproved tools only
ProcedureShows how to complyRecord and review

A framework for getting it right

NIST released the AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023. Crucially, the four functions form a loop, not a one-off checklist.

  • Asked: Organisations ask: how do we manage AI risk in a way that works, day after day?
  • Answer: The US standards body published a framework built on four functions: govern, map, measure, manage

Somebody owns it, somebody records it, and somebody checks it — before deployment, and after.

The benefit system that went wrong

The Dutch data protection authority later fined the tax administration. This is what missing governance looks like at national scale.

  • Asked: Dutch authorities used risk-scoring to flag childcare-benefit fraud, and leaned in part on nationality as a signal
  • Answer: Thousands of innocent families were wrongly pursued; in 2021 the government resigned over the scandal

The governance rhythm

  1. Own: a named person for every system
  2. Register: know what is in use, and where
  3. Review: check before, and keep checking after
  4. Respond: a route to challenge, and to fix

6. A professional decision framework

Sub-area 8.6, the professional decision framework.

The framework is used before you accept the output. Used properly, it costs you a moment, not a procedure.

Six questions, in order

  1. Purpose: is AI the right tool for this task at all?
  2. Permission: does policy allow this use, with this tool?
  3. Protection: is this data safe to put into the system?
  4. Proof: have I checked the output against a source I trust?
  5. Fairness: could this disadvantage a person or group unfairly?
  6. Responsibility: can I explain and own this decision?

The framework in action

Three of the six questions settled it, with no drama and no harm. That is the framework earning its keep — quietly, before anything went wrong.

  • Asked: A staff officer is asked to summarise a stack of reports for a briefing, and thinks of using a public AI tool
  • Answer: Purpose is fine; permission and protection are not. The officer stops, and uses the approved tool instead

From can, to may, to should

  • What you can do — What the tool is technically capable of
  • What you may do — What policy and law permit
  • What you should do — What fairness and your judgement demand

How it fits together

The six questions structure your thinking. They do not replace it. You still decide — and you still own the decision.

Ask before you accept the output. If any answer is a red flag, stop and escalate. Record the answers for consequential work.

Bias is designed in or out. Humans stay accountable. Policy makes both routine — and six quiet questions keep you straight.

Domain 8 in seven lines

  • Bias enters through data, coverage, design and use
  • Accurate is not the same as fair
  • Transparency, explainability and accountability are three duties
  • A human owns every consequential decision
  • Disclose AI use whenever people are affected
  • Governance: a named owner, a register, and review
  • Ask: purpose, permission, protection, proof, fairness, responsibility

Glossary of key terms

Artificial intelligence. Machines performing tasks we associate with human intelligence; in practice, learning patterns from data.

Machine learning. The part of AI that improves from data rather than following rules a person wrote.

Deep learning. Machine learning using layered neural networks.

Generative AI. Deep learning that produces new content: text, images, audio or code.

Narrow AI. A system built for one task or a narrow family of tasks. All deployed AI today is narrow.

General AI. A hypothetical system matching a person across any task. It does not exist today.

Token. A chunk of text, roughly part of a word, that a language model processes.

Context window. The amount of text a model can consider at once.

Knowledge cut-off. The date after which a model has no built-in knowledge.

Embedding. A numeric representation of meaning that lets a model compare items by similarity.

Hallucination. Fluent, confident output that is simply wrong.

Prompt. The instruction you give a model.

Few-shot prompting. Including examples in a prompt to show the model the pattern you want.

Retrieval-augmented generation. Supplying a model with retrieved documents so its answer is grounded in real sources.

Agent. A system that plans and takes multiple steps towards a goal using tools.

Shadow AI. Using unapproved AI tools for official work.

Prompt injection. Hidden instructions inside content that trick a model into ignoring its real task.

Bias. A systematic unfairness in a model's output, usually inherited from its data or design.

Human oversight. A person remaining responsible for consequential decisions.

Disclosure. Telling people when AI has been used to produce something that affects them.

Quick revision checklist

  • I can define AI and say what it is not.
  • I can put AI, machine learning, deep learning and generative AI in the right order.
  • I know training from inference, and why inference never learns.
  • I can explain tokens, the context window and the knowledge cut-off.
  • I can name the four working categories of AI task.
  • I can tell a chatbot from an agent.
  • I can write a prompt with a task, context, format and constraints.
  • I know few-shot prompting and when to decompose a task.
  • I verify facts, figures, sums and citations before they leave.
  • I can spot hallucination, staleness and fabrication.
  • I know the protective ladder and what never goes into a public tool.
  • I can defeat a deepfake or a cloned voice out of band.
  • I can explain bias, transparency, explainability and accountability.
  • I know when to disclose AI use.
  • I can apply the six-question decision framework.
  • I know the exam format: 120 questions, 70 per cent, 120 minutes, closed book.
